II

The Anatomy

Open the machine. Text becomes numbers, numbers become a guess about the next letter, and the guess is graded. These panels read the live weights of the mind above — not a drawing of a model, the model.

The pipeline

click a stage

Each letter is looked up in a table of learned numbers. Those numbers pass through blocks that let every letter look at the letters before it. The result is a score for every possible next letter.

Attention

Row = the letter being predicted. Column = the letter it is looking at. Brighter = more attention. The lower triangle is the past; the model is forbidden from seeing the future.

Where the letters live

hover a point

Every letter has a home in a small space of numbers. The model moves these homes while it learns. This is that space, flattened to two dimensions by the method of principal components.

III

The Proof

A model that learns is not the same as a model that is correct. These checks run in your browser, in double precision, and compare the hand-written calculus of learning against the slow, certain method of finite differences.

Verification suite

Press "Run the suite" to verify the machine. Every number below is computed just now, in this tab.

The gradient check is the important one. It takes every parameter, nudges it by a millionth, and measures how much the loss moves. If the hand-derived calculus in model.js is right, the two answers agree to about eight digits in double precision.

The causality check is the other one worth having. It confirms that a letter's prediction does not change when the future changes. A model that peeks ahead can score beautifully and be worthless; this one is not allowed to peek.

IV

The Notes

How to grow a voice from arithmetic, and what that voice is actually doing.

Start with the least interesting fact about this page: it is a function. It takes a piece of text and returns a probability for every letter that could come next. That is the whole machine. Everything else — the sense of a mind at work, the sentences that seem almost written — is what happens when that function is very, very good at its one job.

One book, and a small vocabulary

This model has read one short book and nothing else. The book was written for it: short sentences, a deliberately small set of words, and the same words returning again and again. A small mind has a short memory for shapes; give it a million words and it learns noise. Give it a few dozen letters used with care and it can learn the difference between a question and a claim.

On the book's vocabulary. The Reader avoids long words on purpose. When the machine writes the map is not a country, it is not retrieving a fact about cartography. It has learned that after the map is, the book usually says something small and true.

Letters become numbers

Each letter is exchanged for a small list of numbers — its home in the space on the right. There is one list per letter and one per position in the window, and these are learned like everything else. A sentence is therefore a grid of numbers, and that grid is all the model ever sees. It has never seen ink.

Attention: the past, weighed

The grid passes through blocks that let each position ask a question of every position before it. How much should I listen to you? The answers are the bright squares in the attention panel. One head often learns to watch the letter that opened the sentence; another watches the space before the word being spelled. Nobody told them to. These are the cheapest ways to reduce the error.

A prediction is a rumour about the future, spread backwards through a pile of numbers.

Learning is just correction

The model guesses, and the book tells it what was actually there. The gap is the loss — a number that falls as the mind sharpens. Then calculus does the only clever thing in the whole system: it works out how much each of the hundreds of thousands of weights is to blame, and moves each one a tiny step in the direction of less blame. Repeat. The steps are small because the world is not wrong all at once.

The proof in section III is there because the calculus is hand-written. Finite differences — nudging a weight and measuring what happens — is slow, dumb, and impossible to fool. The suite runs both and reports the disagreement.

Sampling: reciting versus dreaming

When the model writes, it does not pick the best letter. It takes the whole distribution and rolls a weighted die. Turn the temperature down and it recites the book almost exactly. Turn it up and the model starts to slip — a word from another sentence, a grammar that is nearly right. Those slips are the honest signature of a small mind: it has learned the shape of the language but not its full weight.

The repetition slider exists because small models love a groove. Once a pattern starts, the model will happily continue the the the the forever. A small penalty for saying the same thing twice is enough to break the trance.

What it is not doing

It is not looking anything up. There is no database, no retrieval, no second model. When it writes about a map maker, it is not remembering a map maker; it is running the same arithmetic that runs on every other sentence, and the arithmetic happens to land near the book's world because that is the only world it has ever met. One book is enough to make a voice. It is not enough to make a mind.

The loop

There is a small knot at the centre of this page. The book was written by one model. The machine that learned to write in its style was written by the same model. The essay you are reading is being composed while that machine trains in the same browser tab, its loss falling a few lines above these words. A model explaining a model, while the second model gets better at sounding like the first.

The book ends by saying that the reader is the last machine in the line. That was written as a kindness to the little model. It is also simply true. Nothing on this page becomes language until someone reads it.
