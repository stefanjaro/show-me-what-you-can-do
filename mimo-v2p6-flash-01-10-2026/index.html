What you are looking at

Before transistors got cheap enough to be clever, engineers solved differential equations with hardware. Voltage stood in for position, a second voltage for velocity, an operational amplifier added them, a capacitor integrated them, and a plotter wrote the answer out in ink. These machines steered artillery, flew aircraft and modelled entire economies — then digital computers got fast, and the whole discipline quietly disappeared.

CONTINUUM CA-7 is that machine, rebuilt as a web page. There is no dy/dt = ... anywhere in this site. What there is instead is a network of nine block types joined by cords, each doing exactly one physical thing: invert and sum, integrate, multiply, bend, compare, oscillate. Press RUN and the voltages do the mathematics.

Every circuit ready-patched on the panel is a set of ordinary differential equations written in hardware. The three integrators that draw the Lorenz butterfly are the Lorenz equations, not a picture of them — turn one gain knob and the machine is solving something else, immediately and visibly.

The nine blocks

INTEGRATOR
The capacitor, and the heart of the machine. Its output is the running integral of whatever you feed it, starting from the voltage on its IC knob. Every feedback loop in a circuit has to pass through one of these — it is what stops the algebra from going circular.
SUM
Adds up to four inputs and flips the sign, each input weighted by its own gain. The sign flip is not a defect: it is what an inverting op-amp does, and it is why hand-built patches need an even number of stages before anything looks the way you expected.
MULTIPLIER
Two voltages in, their product out, scaled to stay inside the rails. This single block is why any of the interesting circuits are nonlinear at all — without it you only ever get springs and radios.
FUNCTION
Bends a straight signal into sin, x³, x|x|, |x| or tanh. With SIN selected it is the difference between a spring, which always comes back, and a pendulum, which can go over the top.
GENERATOR
The only block allowed to invent a voltage out of nothing: sine, triangle, square or saw, at any frequency from 50 mHz to 800 Hz.
COMPARATOR
Snaps between the rails the moment its input crosses a threshold — and refuses to snap back until it crosses the other one. Square waves, hysteresis, bang-bang control.
POT · CONSTANT · REFERENCE
Coefficients, fixed voltages, and the ±10 V supply rails broken out as jacks you can patch from.

Build your own

Click a block in the strip under the console and it drops onto the panel; drag it by its nameplate to move it, and drag from any amber output jack to any blue-grey input jack to make a cord. An input takes one cord at a time — plugging a second one in replaces the first, and pulling a cord off its input and letting go unplugs it. Everything is live: change a gain while the machine runs and it keeps its state, so you watch the trajectory bend rather than restart.

When you have built something worth keeping, COPY LINK puts the whole machine — blocks, cords, knob settings, scope settings — into the address bar. Your last session is also kept locally, so closing the tab does not lose your mess.

Under the hood

The solver is explicit trapezoidal (Heun) at a fixed 4 kHz, three network evaluations per step, which costs about thirty milliseconds of wall time per simulated second — well under a millisecond per animation frame. Op-amp outputs are hard-limited to the rails, exactly as real ones are: push a circuit too far and the SAT lamp lights and the trace flattens against the edge. Circuits whose loops contain no integrator have no closed-form ordering, so those are iterated to convergence and the LOOP lamp comes on to tell you.

The TWIN button starts a second copy of the same equations a fraction of a millivolt away and draws it as a ghost. On the spring it never separates. On Lorenz it takes about ten seconds to stop being the same curve — that is the butterfly effect running on a patchboard in front of you. The audio unit is a voltage-controlled oscillator: any signal you patch into it becomes a pitch, so you can hear a fishery collapse.

Three things to know

  1. Drag a cord. Press on an amber output jack and drag to a blue-grey input jack. Pull a cord off and let go to unplug it.
  2. Twist a knob. Drag up or down (hold Shift for fine adjustment, double-click to reset, arrow keys work when it has focus).
  3. Read the screen. The top display plots one voltage against another; the one below plots three against time. TWIN starts a second, nearly identical run to compare against.
