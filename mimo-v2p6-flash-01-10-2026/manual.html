1 · Voltage as a variable

An analog computer has no idea what a number is. It has voltages, and rules about how voltages may change. If you decide that 1 volt means 1 centimetre of displacement, then a wire carrying 3.4 volts is 3.4 centimetres, everywhere on the panel at once, continuously, forever. Addition happens because currents add at a junction. Multiplication happens because a field effect transistor's channel narrows with one voltage and carries another. Integration — the hard one, the one no resistor can do — happens because charge piles up on a capacitor at a rate proportional to the current pushing it.

1.1 The inverting summer

Every op-amp block on this panel is built around the same trick. The amplifier's inverting input is a virtual ground: negative feedback drives it to whatever voltage sits on the non-inverting input, which is ground. So the input currents never fight each other — they all pour into a node that isn't moving, and the amplifier must sink exactly that current through its feedback resistor. The output therefore swings to the negative of the weighted sum:

Vout = −( g1V1 + g2V2 + g3V3 + g4V4 )

The minus sign is not a design flaw, it is the whole design. It is what makes the feedback negative instead of a latch. Flip the sign twice and you get back where you started; flip it an odd number of times and every equation you write down needs its signs reversed. Section 3 makes this a rule you can follow without thinking.

1.2 The integrator

Replace the feedback resistor with a capacitor and the block stops being a summer and starts being calculus. Since i = C dv/dt and the summing junction keeps the input current proportional to the input voltage, the output voltage becomes the running integral:

Vout(t) = IC − ∫0t ( g1V1 + g2V2 + g3V3 ) dτ

The IC knob sets the charge already on the capacitor: the value the integral starts from. Press IC RESET and every integrator on the panel snaps back to its initial condition, which is how a real machine starts a computation. It is also the only place on this panel where a number is stored. Nothing else holds anything — take your hands off the knobs and every other block simply reports what its inputs are doing this instant.

3 · Rules of the patchboard

  1. Every loop must pass through an integrator. A circle of summers and multipliers defines an algebraic equation with no way to evaluate it; the machine reports LOOP and iterates to a best guess. A circle containing an integrator defines a differential equation, which is the only thing this machine is good for.
  2. Count your inversions. SUM and INTEGRATOR each negate. Two of them in a row cancel; one on its own flips the sign of everything downstream. If your trajectory runs away in the direction you didn't expect, you almost certainly owe the equation a sign.
  3. Keep everything inside ±10 V. Op‑amps saturate against their supplies, and so does this one. The SAT lamp means a block is pinned to a rail and no longer doing arithmetic — rescale your variables, or lower a coefficient.
  4. The multiplier divides by ten. OUT = g·A·B/10 exists so that two 10‑volt signals can be multiplied without leaving the rails. The g knob is your output potentiometer; use it to put the product back into the units your equation wants.
  5. Every state variable gets its own integrator. A second‑order equation needs two, wired head to tail. Three‑dimensional chaos needs three.
  6. An open input is 0 V. Nothing is connected means the term isn't there — which is how you turn a coefficient off, and how you leave an unused comparator input at reference.
  7. Initial conditions are part of the problem. The IC knob is not a preference; it is the value of the state at t = 0. Set it, press IC RESET, and the trajectory begins again from exactly there — which is how you demonstrate sensitive dependence by starting two runs a hair apart.
  8. Scale before you wire. Real machines never put a physical quantity on the panel at full magnitude; they solve in machine units and convert afterwards. Section 4 shows the arithmetic on the Lorenz circuit.

4 · Two worked derivations

4.1 A mass on a spring

Start from the equation and count what you need. ẍ = −kx is second order, so it wants two integrators: one that turns acceleration into velocity, one that turns velocity into position.

ẍ = −kx  ⇒  v = ∫(−kx) dt  ⇒  x = ∫(v) dt

Now the signs. An integrator computes OUT = IC − ∫(...), so the expression in the brackets must be the negative of the derivative you want:

WireGainWhy
v.OUT → x.1−1 x' = v requires the bracket to equal −v.
x.OUT → v.1+k v' = −kx requires the bracket to equal +kx.

One gain is negative, one is positive: the two inversions are what make the pair agree with a single −kx. Turn up the pot in the feedback path and you add a term +c·v to the bracket, which is −c·v to the derivative — friction, for the price of one patch cord.

5 · Numerical notes

Of course the machine is digital underneath — a real op‑amp does not have a clock. What matters is that the clock is fast, the method is honest, and every approximation is one you can find on this page.

MethodExplicit trapezoidal (Heun, RK2): predict with a forward Euler step, re‑evaluate the network, then take the average of the two slopes.
Timestep1/4000 s exactly. Three network evaluations per step (predict, correct, and a final evaluation so the displays read the settled values).
Order of evaluationDependencies are topologically sorted. Integrator outputs are state, so they are treated as sources — which is exactly why every loop needs to pass through one. If a cycle survives that reduction, the algebra has no closed‑form solution: the network is iterated six times to convergence and the LOOP lamp comes on.
RailsEvery block output is limited to ±10 V. The lamp is only lit when a value would have exceeded a rail, so sitting exactly on one (a stock that has reached carrying capacity, say) does not count as a fault.
Display samplingEvery tenth step — 400 samples per second — into ring buffers of 8192 samples, i.e. 20.5 seconds of history per watched signal. The screens redraw once per animation frame; the XY tube decays older points into the phosphor, the time base rescales each channel automatically to 1, 2, 5 or 10 V/div.
Twin runA second copy of the network, same cords, same knobs, initial conditions offset by a fixed epsilon: 0.0004 V for Lorenz, 0.001 V for Rössler and the driven pendulum. It costs nothing to run and it is the butterfly effect as a feature.
CostAbout 30 ms of wall time per simulated second — under half a millisecond per animation frame on an ordinary laptop.

5.1 Verification

None of the ready‑patched circuits were trusted on sight. Each one is checked by node test/run.js against an independently written RK4 integration of the equations printed on its panel, together with physical invariants: energy conservation for the spring, the conserved quantity of Lotka–Volterra, the equilibrium of the fishery at two levels of effort, the population sum and monotone decline of the epidemic, the exact period of the relaxation oscillator, the amplitude of the van der Pol limit cycle, and statistics for the chaotic systems (including how far the twin runs apart, and by when). Seventy‑six assertions, all of them passing. node test/ui.js then drives the real page in headless Chromium: it drags cords in and out, turns knobs, builds and deletes blocks, shares a link and reloads it, exercises the transport and checks a phone‑s width.

6 · The audio monitor

Sound is just a voltage that changes fast enough to be interesting. The AUDIO unit has two modes and nothing starts until you press ENABLE AUDIO, because browsers require a human gesture before they will open a sound card.

VCO
Any signal you patch in becomes a pitch: f = 110 Hz · 22.5V/10. Zero volts is 110 Hz, +10 V is 622 Hz, −10 V drops to 19 Hz. The oscillator is a triangle, and its frequency follows the voltage through a 25 ms smoothing filter so it glides instead of stepping. Because the pitch tracks the signal rather than the waveform, a slowly evolving curve — the fishery collapsing, an epidemic peaking — reads as a melody you can follow.
SIGNAL
The voltage itself, played as audio. The solver hands every 4 kHz sample of the patched signal to an AudioWorklet, which resamples it to the device rate with linear interpolation and holds the last value if the machine is held — a sample and hold, exactly as it should be. Change SPEED and the pitch changes with it: you are playing the simulation back at a different tape speed.

7.1 When something looks wrong

Nothing movesHOLD is lit? A cord missing from the scope? Are the ICs where you think? Integrators only change when something feeds them.
SAT is litA block is pinned to a rail. Turn a coefficient down, or scale the variables (rule 8 of section 3).
LOOP is litYou have wired a loop with no integrator in it. Break it, or put an integrator in it.
The screens are blankThey are inputs, not displays: patch a cord into X and Y for the phase plot, CH1–3 for the time base.
No soundPress ENABLE AUDIO once; the browser will only allow that from a real click. Then check that a cord reaches the AUDIO unit's IN jack.
