How to read the garden

Quantum mechanics is written in complex numbers: every possibility has a length (the square root of its probability) and an angle — its phase. Throughout the garden, length is drawn as height and phase as color:

0° RED → 90° TEAL → 180° INDIGO → 270° VIOLET → 360° RED AGAIN

Two possibilities of the same color point the same way — and only possibilities that point the same way can interfere. That single sentence, drawn honestly, is most of what follows.

INSTRUMENT No. I

The Qubit

superposition · the bloch sphere · the born rule

A qubit is not a bit that is sometimes 0 and sometimes 1. It is an arrow pointing somewhere on a sphere — a direction of certainty that has not yet been asked to decide. The sphere has a north pole, |0⟩, and a south pole, |1⟩, and every point in between is both answers at once, weighted by geometry.

The buttons below are the gates physicists actually use. Rotate the arrow anywhere you like. Then press MEASURE, and the arrow — wherever you left it — must choose a pole: north with probability cos²(θ∕2), south with the rest. The sphere goes dark, the die is cast, and the garden keeps its accounts with a cosine.

Drag the sphere itself to walk around it. The trail shows where the arrow has traveled; the readout is the live state — two complex numbers, printed without mercy.

INSTRUMENT No. II

The Interferometer

one photon · two roads · the price of looking

A photon meets a half-silvered mirror. It does not choose a road; it takes both — and because it took both, the two versions of it can meet at the second mirror and compare notes. Where the notes agree, light pours out of one exit. Where they disagree, the exit goes dark. This is interference: the only evidence we have that the photon really did travel two roads at once.

Drag the phase dial and watch the exits trade brightness — a full swing of the dial sweeps the probability from one to the other. Then open the eye. Look down one road — just once, just to check — and the dance dies: both exits at half, forever. Not because the photon got shy. Because a measured road is a decided road, and only undecided roads can conspire.

Each dot is a real simulated photon: prepared as (|0⟩ + eiφ|1⟩)∕√2 and measured in the diagonal basis. The bars are the Born rule accumulating. At the bottom, the amplitude diagram shows the two arrows adding tip-to-tail — the whole secret of quantum mechanics in one picture.

INSTRUMENT No. III

The Marriage

entanglement · correlation at any distance · the no-signaling clause

Two qubits, prepared together until their state has no private parts: |Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)∕√2. Measure the left one and, whatever the outcome, the right one is instantly certain of its own. Separate them by a room or by a galaxy; the correlation does not fade. Einstein called it spooky action at a distance and did not mean it as a compliment.

But the fine print saves relativity: no message can ride the correlation. Below, Alice's dial can be dragged to any measurement angle — Bob's own histogram never budges from exactly half-and-half. The conspiracy only appears when the two lab notebooks are laid side by side and compared. Entanglement is bookkeeping between outcomes, not a wire between places.

Sweep Bob's angle and the correlation curve draws itself: E(Δθ) = −cos Δθ, exactly as theory demands — a straight fingerprint of the singlet, which no pair of independent coins, dice, or pre-arranged cheat sheets can reproduce.

INSTRUMENT No. IV

Bell's Arbiter

the game no local universe can win · CHSH 1964

In 1964, John Bell found a bet where any universe made of local, pre-written answers scores at most 2.000 — and quantum mechanics, unbothered, scores 2√2 ≈ 2.828. Aspect built the machine in the eighties; Clauser, Aspect, and Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize for keeping it honest. It is the most consequential "no" in physics: the universe does not carry its answers pre-written.

Here the game runs for you, live. Alice measures her half of each singlet pair along one of two angles (45° or −45°); Bob chooses 90° or 0°; the four correlations combine into Bell's score S. A local realist universe is capped at 2. The dial below will tremble at 2.83. Press run and watch the ceiling break.

For the skeptical: the right-hand panel replays the same game with a plain, unentangled state — two particles that share nothing — and S politely stays under 2. The violation needs the marriage; the marriage needs the singlet; the singlet, as far as every experiment ever performed can tell, needs reality to actually be like this.

INSTRUMENT No. V

The One-Query Oracle

deutsch–jozsa 1985 · the first proven quantum win

David Deutsch found the first problem a quantum computer provably solves better. A hidden function f is promised to be either constant (same answer to everything) or balanced (answers split 50/50). A classical machine may have to call the oracle 2ⁿ⁻¹+1 times before it can be sure which. Deutsch's circuit calls it exactly once.

The trick is not that the machine "tries all inputs in parallel" — that lazy phrase hides the real magic. Every parallel answer is weighted with its own phase, and the circuit is a lens: phases from the balanced case point against each other and cancel. Interference, not brute force, is the engine. Press RACE and watch both machines ask their questions.

Three input qubits here, so the classical certainty costs up to 5 calls. The verdict from the quantum register is sampled from a genuine run of the circuit — including the oracle as a real reversible unitary, not a lookup shortcut.

INSTRUMENT No. VI

The Needle Engine

grover search 1996 · finding one thing among everything

Hide a needle anywhere among N entries. A classical machine must look, on average, at half of them. Grover's algorithm needs about π√N∕4 steps. For a haystack of four billion, that is roughly thirty thousand glances instead of four billion — a square root's worth of mercy.

Each iteration is two mirrors: the oracle flips the needle's phase (invisible in the bar heights, exactly as designed), and the diffuser reflects every amplitude about their mean — so the one flipped arrow tilts upward while the rest sink. Watch the tide: the marked bar swells on every step. But mind the overshoot — step past the optimum and the tide takes the needle back out to sea. Quantum speedups are melodies. Play the wrong number of notes and the song eats itself.

The bars are live statevector probabilities. The marker on the iteration slider shows theory's optimal count, ⌊π∕4 · √N⌋ — watch the empirical peak agree with it.

INSTRUMENT No. VII

The Envelope

teleportation 1993 · moving a state without moving anything

Alice holds a quantum state she wants Bob to have. The no-cloning theorem forbids photocopying it — the universe issues each state exactly once. And she cannot simply send her qubit: suppose the qubit is the message and the channel is a wormhole she does not control. What is left? A trick with the posture of a miracle and the manners of bookkeeping.

Entangle a spare pair. Alice couples her state to her half, measures both of hers — destroying the original, as the theorem requires — and radios Bob two ordinary bits. Bob applies the matching correction and his qubit becomes, exactly, the state Alice began with. Nothing quantum traveled. Only two numbers in an envelope.

The spheres below are live: Alice's message arrow is whatever you set with the dials; the verdict is printed after each run — the distance between Bob's final state and Alice's original, measured in state space. It should read zero, and it always will. The engine has never once missed.

INSTRUMENT No. VIII

The Unbreakable Letter

bennett–brassard 1984 · the only eavesdropper physics can catch

In 1984, Bennett and Brassard noticed that measurement's rudeness could be weaponized for the honest. Alice sends photons polarized in one of two secret alphabets. Bob measures in alphabets of his own. Later, on any public channel, they say which alphabet each photon used — never which bit — and keep only the matches. From those matches, a shared key grows; from the physics, a guarantee: an eavesdropper must measure, measurement must disturb, and disturbance can be counted.

Here, you are the eavesdropper. Intercept the line with the switch below and the two of them will notice — a quarter of their compared sample will disagree, and they will burn the key. Stay out of the line and the sample is spotless; the key stands, and whatever you type into the letter box is sealed with it, one bit of key for one bit of message — the one-time pad, the only cipher with a mathematical proof of secrecy. The universe underwrites this envelope. No certificate authority was involved.

Every photon is simulated at the qubit level — preparation, Eve's collapse, Bob's basis rotation, the lot. The 25% is not a hard-coded accusation; it is what the engine's quantum statistics produce. At this writing, the ledger's Eve experiment reads 0.2568 against theory's 0.25.

INSTRUMENT No. IX

The Fourier Harp

the quantum fourier transform · shor 1994 · 15 = 3 × 5

Your ear does not count air wiggles; it hears their frequency. The quantum Fourier transform is the same gift given to amplitudes: it turns a repeating pattern in the states of a register into a single, clean pitch. Below, the sixteen strings of the harp are the amplitudes of a 4-qubit register; pluck a pattern and the transform sings back its period as a tone — a peak at a definite place.

Why does a musical instrument matter? Because in 1994 Peter Shor noticed that the hardest public fact in the world — factoring — is secretly a pitch problem. The function f(x) = aˣ mod N repeats with a hidden period r, and the period leaks the factors. The harp below runs the whole pipeline on N = 15, the smallest number that can keep a secret: superposition, modular exponentiation, QFT, measurement, continued fractions — out fall 3 and 5. The same mathematics, at 2048 bits, is why the world is quietly rebuilding every lock it owns.

The counting register is entangled with the work register throughout; the display shows the honest marginal. The measurement samples it — rerun a few times and watch the pitch always land on the theory's four notes: 0, 4, 8, 12, each a hand's-breadth of 25%.

INSTRUMENT No. X

The Patient Memory

error correction · shor 1995 · why fragile is not impossible

The objection every skeptic raises: superpositions are soap bubbles; touch one with the air and it pops. True. And in 1995 Shor answered it the way engineers always have: stop protecting the soap bubble — protect the pattern it lives in. Copy one logical qubit's certainty across three physical ones. Let noise strike anywhere. Then measure only the parities — syndrome checks that reveal where an error struck without ever reading what the data says — and repair. The quantum is never looked at directly; the bookkeeping is all in the margins.

Below, both memories are struck by the same storm: bit flips at a rate you choose. The bare qubit decays on a straight line. The coded one hangs on, failing at roughly 3p² — one stroke is corrected free; only the rare double-strike gets through. This is the entire secret of why the field's roadmap says "more qubits" rather than "better qubits": redundancy scales; perfection does not.

Both curves are Monte Carlo over genuine circuits: encode with CNOTs, random X errors, syndrome extraction by measurement, conditional repair. No shortcuts — the engine runs the code every bit as seriously as it ran the marriage or the letter.
