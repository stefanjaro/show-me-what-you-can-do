The cabinet

Six ways of thinking,
made of wood, brass and wire.

A calculating machine is an argument made solid. It takes a piece of reasoning — place value, a logarithm, a polynomial, a substitution — and turns it into a shape you can touch. The shape then does the thinking for you.

These six are working models, not pictures. Every bead, gear, rotor and relay is drawn and calculated here, in your browser, from scratch. Turn the cranks. Type a message. Make the machines work.

No videos. No pre-rendered images. No libraries. Everything below is live.

I
IChina · before 1600

The Counting
Frame

Ten fingers, given places to stand.

The suanpan is a place-value machine you can hear. Each rod carries two beads worth five and five beads worth one. Slide them to the beam and the frame holds a number — not as a symbol, but as a position. Merchants, clerks and astronomers used it for centuries; a skilled hand can beat a pocket calculator.

How it works

Each rod is a decimal place. A bead above the beam is worth 5; each bead below the beam is worth 1. The value on a rod is the sum of the beads touching the beam. The frame can hold intermediate values above 9, which is useful while carrying. The number shown is the sum of every rod's place value.

Try this. Set the frame to 3,908 and watch the beads find it. Then clear it and try to beat the machine by hand.

II
IIAntikythera · Greece · c. 150 BCE

The Bronze
Sky

A computer built to keep the heavens in order.

In 1901, sponge divers brought up a lump of corroded bronze from a shipwreck near Antikythera. Inside were at least thirty gearwheels, cut with teeth about a millimetre long. The machine followed the Sun and Moon through the zodiac, counted the Metonic and Saros cycles, and predicted eclipses. Nothing else of comparable mechanical complexity survives from the ancient world.

What you are seeing

This is an interactive reconstruction of the dials and cycles, not a replica of the surviving fragments. The sky is computed with low-precision astronomical formulae. The front dial shows the Sun and Moon; the rear spirals count lunar months and mark eclipse possibilities. Gear tooth counts mentioned in the notes come from published reconstructions. Drag the dial or use the slider to move through time.

Try this. Crank the sky forward and watch the Moon's phase turn. Press “Next eclipse” and see when the dragon's mouth opens.

III
IIIEngland · 1622

The
Slipstick

Multiplication, folded into a ruler.

William Oughtred placed two logarithmic scales side by side and gave the world a pocket-sized multiplier. To multiply, you slide one scale until its index meets the first number, then read the second number's position against the fixed scale. The answer appears as a distance. Engineers used slide rules to design bridges, aircraft and the Apollo missions; the last slide rules were made in the 1970s.

How it works

On a logarithmic scale, equal distances are equal ratios. Adding two distances multiplies the numbers. The C and D scales each span one decade; the A and B scales span two. The runner marks a position across the scales. A slide rule gives about three significant figures — it is an instrument of estimation, not exact arithmetic.

Try this. Multiply 3.42 by 7.6. Then square 8.9, or take the square root of 57. Drag the slide and the runner yourself.

IV
IVEngland · 1822

The Difference
Engine

A machine for the unglamorous work of tables.

Charles Babbage designed an engine to compute and print mathematical tables without human error. His trick was the finite difference: for any polynomial, the differences between successive values eventually become constant. Feed the machine the first few values, and it can produce the rest by repeated addition — no multiplication required. Babbage's engine was never finished in his lifetime; a complete difference engine was built by the Scheutz brothers in 1853.

How it works

For a cubic f(n) = a n³ + b n² + c n + d, the first values and their differences are set on the columns. Each turn of the crank adds the first difference to the value, then adds the second difference to the first, and so on. The rightmost difference stays constant. The printed table is a polynomial, produced one addition at a time.

Try this. Set a, b, c, d to 0, 1, 0, 0 for squares; 0, 0, 1, 0 for a line; or 1, 0, 0, 0 for cubes. Turn the crank and watch the columns settle.

V
VGermany · 1918

The Enigma
Machine

A secret that changed every time you pressed a key.

The Enigma was an electric cipher machine: a keyboard, a lampboard, a plugboard and a set of rotating wired wheels. Every keypress sent a current through the wheels and back, lighting a different letter. The rotors stepped with each press, so the same letter never encrypted the same way twice. The German military used it for its most secret traffic. Polish cryptanalysts broke early versions; at Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and his colleagues built machines that broke the rest.

Why it fell

Enigma had flaws. A letter could never encrypt to itself, which gave codebreakers a foothold. The rotors' double-step created predictable patterns. Most of all, operators repeated messages and used predictable keys. The cipher's keyspace was enormous, but the machine still leaked. This exhibit implements the historical rotor wirings, reflector, plugboard and stepping, including the double step. It is a teaching model, not a secure cipher.

Try this. Type a message on the keyboard below. Then press “Use ciphertext” and run it again with the same settings — Enigma is reciprocal, so the ciphertext turns back into plaintext. Move a rotor and watch the path change.

VI
VIUnited States · 1937

The Relay
Adder

Logic you can hear.

Before transistors, computers thought with relays: coils that pulled metal contacts open and closed. In 1937, Claude Shannon showed that Boolean algebra could design these circuits — that a network of switches could compute any logical expression. Four full adders in a row make a ripple-carry adder: the carry from one bit wakes the next, like a row of falling dominoes. Early machines such as the Z3 and the Harvard Mark I used relays; they were slow, loud and wonderfully legible.

How it works

Each full adder takes two input bits and a carry, and produces a sum and a carry out. Sum = A XOR B XOR Cin. Carry out = (A AND B) OR (Cin AND (A XOR B)). In subtraction mode the B inputs are inverted and the first carry is set to 1 — two's complement. Watch the carry ripple from the least significant bit to the most significant.

Try this. Set A = 9 and B = 5. Watch the carry travel. Then switch to subtract and try 5 − 9: the result is negative, and the top lamp shows the borrow.

Notes & sources

Small print for large machines.

Antikythera. The exhibit is an interactive reconstruction of the dials and cycles, not a replica of the surviving fragments. Celestial positions use low-precision formulae; eclipse marks are possibilities, not predictions. Tooth counts and cycle lengths follow published reconstructions, including Freeth et al. (2006, 2021) and the 2024 calendar-ring study.

Enigma. Historical rotor wirings, reflector B/C, plugboard and stepping are implemented, including the double step. The machine is a teaching model, not a secure cipher. The real Enigma I had five rotors and up to ten plugboard pairs; this model follows that scheme.

Difference engine. The simulation uses the finite-difference method Babbage designed for. The physical engine was never completed in his lifetime; the first complete difference engine was built by the Scheutz brothers in 1853.

What is not here. No external libraries, no images, no video, no network requests at runtime. The typefaces are self-hosted. Every diagram, gear, bead and lamp is drawn with Canvas 2D; every calculation runs in JavaScript on your machine.

Typefaces. Cinzel, Cormorant Garamond and IBM Plex Mono are self-hosted under the SIL Open Font License; the licence text sits in fonts/OFL.txt.

Accuracy. This is a cabinet of working ideas, not a metrology lab. The mechanisms are simplified where history is uncertain and labeled where they are approximations. The pleasure is in the motion, the arithmetic and the long conversation between them.
